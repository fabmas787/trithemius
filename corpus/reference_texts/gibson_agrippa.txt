AGRIPPA (A BOOK OF THE DEAD)
By William Gibson (1992)

I hesitated
before the black album.
The album had been a gift
from my father's father.
He had bought it in Wheeling, West Virginia,
and brought it home across the mountains
in the rumble-seat of a Buick.
It was covered in imitation morocco,
stamped with the trademark of an Eastman Kodak company.
Inside were pages of heavy black paper,
each bound with a cord of twisted silk.
The corners of the pages had been protected
with tiny triangles of gummed black paper,
holding the photographs in place.
Most of the photographs had fallen out,
or been removed by hands
that had themselves ceased to exist.
Those that remained were portraits
of men in celluloid collars,
women in shirtwaists and high-buttoned shoes,
children in sailor suits.
There was a snapshot of a bridge
over an unknown river,
the girders stark against a pale sky.
There was a picture of a street
in a town whose name had been forgotten,
the storefronts bearing signs
in an alphabet that seemed familiar
yet could not be read.
And there was a picture of a dog,
a terrier of some kind,
sitting on a wooden porch
in the sunshine of an afternoon
seventy years before.

The mechanism of the camera
had frozen that moment,
preserved the grain of the weathered pine boards,
the reflection of the sky
in the dog's dark eye,
the dust motes floating in the air.
And then the shutter had closed,
and time had moved on.
The dog had died;
the porch had rotted away;
the house had been torn down;
the town had changed its name;
the century had ended.
Only the image remained,
a ghost etched in silver salts
on a piece of fragile paper.

I turned the page.
There was an envelope glued to the black paper,
an old manila envelope
with a metal clasp.
Inside were documents:
a receipt for a burial plot
in a cemetery in Ohio;
a certificate of discharge
from the United States Army,
dated 1919;
a newspaper clipping
about a train derailment
near the town of Bluefield;
a handwritten recipe
for cough syrup,
written in faded purple ink
on the back of a telegraph form.

The handwriting was my father's father's,
the strokes firm and regular,
the script of a man who had been taught
to write with a steel-nibbed pen
dipped in an inkwell.
He had worked for the railroad,
a clerk in the freight office,
tallying carloads of coal and lumber,
until the day the company failed
and the office closed forever.
He had taken the train home,
walked up the gravel road to his house,
sat down in the kitchen,
and never spoken of the railroad again.

In the bottom of the trunk
beneath the album
was a cigar box made of cedar,
with the brand name stamped in gold:
"El Roi-Tan, the Cigar that Breathes."
Inside the box were copper tokens,
transit tokens from a city
that had dismantled its streetcars
forty years before;
a pocket knife with mother-of-pearl scales,
one of the blades broken;
a pair of gold-rimmed spectacles
with circular lenses,
wrapped in a piece of chamois cloth;
and a brass key with no tag,
whose lock had long since disappeared.

I picked up the spectacles
and held them to my eyes.
The room blurred,
the light refracted into rainbows,
the sharp edges of the furniture
dissolving into luminous haze.
Through those lenses
my grandfather had looked at the world,
had seen the century turn,
had watched the horses give way to automobiles,
the dirt roads paved with asphalt,
the telegraph poles replaced by power lines.
He had seen the Great War begin and end,
the crash of twenty-nine,
the breadlines, the dust storms,
the coming of the new machines.
And then his vision had failed,
the cataracts had formed,
and the world had faded
into the white mist of memory.

The boy in the photograph
is walking toward the camera,
down a path bordered by rhododendrons.
He is wearing knickers and high wool socks,
and his hair is cut short in the style
of the nineteen-twenties.
He is smiling,
a hesitant smile that might be an apology
for being caught in the frame,
or a greeting to someone
who is standing just behind the photographer,
someone who has called his name.
Behind him, the woods rise steeply,
the second-growth oak and chestnut
shrouding the hillside in shadow.
A cool wind is blowing through the gap,
rustling the dry leaves,
carrying the scent of woodsmoke
and damp earth.
In another second he will reach the edge of the frame,
step out of the photograph,
and vanish into the afternoon.

The album closes with a soft thump,
the black paper settling in the dark,
the photographs pressed together
face to face,
the dead whispering to the dead
in the silence of the cedar trunk.
The light from the window fades;
the screen goes blank;
the memory is erased.
A cold wind is coming down the valley,
and the day is done.
